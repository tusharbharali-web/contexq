## Noticing a problem is not the same as knowing what to do about it.

Four questions teams face after an alert:
- Is this real? An alert or early warning arrives on its own. Before anyone can act, they need the records, relationships and history that explain it.
- Where is the evidence? It sits across internal systems and outside sources, in different formats. Gathering it by hand is slow, and the picture is often incomplete.
- What should we do? Several responses may be reasonable. Without the reasoning laid out, teams decide inconsistently or wait for more certainty.
- Can we explain it later? When a decision is reviewed, the evidence and judgement behind it need to be clear, not reconstructed from emails.

## One early warning, from evidence to action.

A walkthrough example in three stages:
1. Investigate: See the evidence, already connected.
2. Recommend: A next step, with its reasons.
3. Act: A person approves, a team follows through.

Early warning: Key supplier of a borrower named in insolvency news.

Stage 1, Investigate (evidence and its source):
- Payments to that supplier are rising (source: Your transactions)
- The two companies share a director (source: Company registry)
- The supplier has filed for insolvency (source: News report)
- Latest accounts not received yet (Still missing)

Stage 2, Recommend:
- Suggested next step: Bring the credit review forward and ask for updated accounts.
- Reasoning: Because payments are rising, a director is shared and the supplier has filed for insolvency. May change when the accounts arrive.
- Other options considered: Keep monitoring; Escalate now.

Stage 3, Act:
- Approved by the credit officer
- Handed to the credit risk team
- Waiting for updated accounts
- Decision and evidence are saved together for later review.

## What changes for the organisation.

- Investigations start further ahead: Analysts begin with connected records, relationships and chronology instead of assembling them, and spend their time on judgement.
- More consistent decisions: Seeing the options and the reasoning side by side helps different people reach comparable decisions on comparable cases.
- Earlier, better-informed action: Teams can act on an early warning while making clear what is still unknown, rather than waiting for complete certainty.
- Decisions you can explain: Evidence, reasoning and approval are kept together for committees, auditors and regulators.
