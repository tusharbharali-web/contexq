## Business records contain only part of the picture.

An accurate record can still miss the wider picture:
- Two borrowers may answer to the same owner.
- Several suppliers may rely on the same upstream factory.
- Payments that look routine one by one may travel in a circle.

Illustrative example, "What one record shows, and what sits around it":
- Customer record: A logistics company. Customer since 2019. Sector: Freight and warehousing. Internal rating: Low risk. Payments: Up to date.
- What sits around it: Ownership: 74% owned by a holding company. Shared owner: The same holding company also owns one of your key suppliers. People: Shares a director with a company named in adverse news.

## First, know who is who. Then see how they relate.

### Entity resolution
Recognise records that belong to the same person, company or account. Keep distinct entities apart, even when they look alike.

Example: three records from different systems, "Company A Ltd" (Customer system), "COMPANY A LIMITED" (Company registry) and "Comp. A (Pty)" (Payment records), resolve into one entity, "Company A", with its identifiers, accounts and history.

Kept apart, two people with the same name: Person 1, company director, born 1971; Person 2, account holder, born 1988. They are not the same entity.

### Knowledge graph
Map the relationships between those entities, then follow connections beyond the first record. Example graph: your customer is owned by a holding company, whose director is shown ("director of owner"); your customer supplies a retail buyer, holds a bank account, pays a shipping partner and is registered at a registered address.

## Build the graph. Analyse it. Explore it.

From connected data to findings your teams can act on.

### Turn records into a connected network.
Build ownership, trading and payment networks around your business. Keep them current as new information arrives.

What the graph can include:
- Ownership chains through to the ultimate beneficial owner (UBO)
- Supplier and customer ecosystems, including alternative suppliers
- Transaction and payment networks between accounts
- Shared directors, addresses and contact details
- Sanctions, adverse news and ESG signals linked to entities

Illustration (records to graph): Holding co. owns 74% of Customer; Holding co. owns 61% of Supplier; Director is director of Holding co.; Customer pays Shipper; Supplier ships with Shipper.

### Find patterns no single record reveals.
Analyse network structure, timing and flows. Prioritise findings so teams know where to investigate first.

How findings are assessed:
- Structure: influential entities and unusual connections.
- Timing: changes in activity and transaction pace.
- Flows: the direction and sequence of movement.

Industry-specific scores combine network findings with financial, ESG, sanctions and operational risk.

Six patterns graph analytics can surface:
- Shared ownership: Separate customers or suppliers controlled by the same parent.
- Indirect exposure: Risk that reaches you through other companies, not a direct link.
- Circular flows: Funds that move through several accounts and return to where they began.
- Rapid pass-through: Money that arrives and leaves an account within hours.
- Fan-in and fan-out: Many senders paying one account, or one account paying many.
- Concentrated dependency: Several suppliers relying on the same upstream source.

### Explore the network, not a spreadsheet.
Start with a company or account. Follow its connections to see how a relationship formed and where it leads. Illustration: "A company group, network over time", shown at 2022, 2024 and Today.

## Where connected context changes the decision.

One graph foundation. Different business decisions. Five applications:

### Credit and group exposure
Question: Which borrowers share a group, and what is our combined exposure?
- What the graph shows: Ownership chains linking seemingly separate borrowers and counterparties.
- What your team can do: Assess lending and trade exposure across the group.
- For: credit risk, corporate banking and portfolio management.

### Financial crime and fraud
Question: Is this account part of a coordinated network?
- What the graph shows: Payment patterns viewed alongside ownership and watchlist information.
- What your team can do: Prioritise connected activity and document the evidence behind each case.
- For: AML, fraud and financial crime teams.

### Supplier and supply-chain risk
Question: Where do suppliers share a dependency, and what could disruption affect?
- What the graph shows: Dependencies beyond tier 1 and exposure to climate, geopolitical and operational events.
- What your team can do: Assess potential disruption and identify alternative suppliers.
- For: procurement, supply-chain and operations leaders.

### KYC, third-party and ESG due diligence
Question: What don't we know about the companies we work with?
- What the graph shows: Sanctions, adverse news and ESG signals linked to the UBOs, directors and affiliates behind each counterparty.
- What your team can do: Review counterparties with their ownership and affiliations in view.
- For: compliance, KYC and sustainability teams.

### Customer and market opportunity
Question: Which businesses connected to our customers could we serve?
- What the graph shows: Customer groups, trading partners and market context.
- What your team can do: Identify relevant prospects and approach them with context.
- For: commercial, relationship management and strategy teams.
